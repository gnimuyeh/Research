# Learning Path: Imperial Chinese History (220–1912 CE)

This curriculum is designed for a motivated learner with no prior background in Chinese history. It takes you from zero to a sophisticated, research-literate understanding of the full imperial period. Estimated time: 12–18 months of regular study.

---

## Prerequisites

Before diving into this period, you will benefit from basic familiarity with:

1. **Early Chinese civilization**: The Shang, Zhou, and Warring States periods; Confucius, the Hundred Schools of Thought, Legalism, Daoism
2. **The Qin and Han dynasties (221 BCE–220 CE)**: The founding of the imperial system, the civil service examination's precursors, the Silk Road's origins, the Han-Xiongnu wars
3. **Basic world geography**: Know where China, Central Asia, Mongolia, Korea, Japan, and the Indian subcontinent are

**Best prerequisite resource**: Patricia Buckley Ebrey, *The Cambridge Illustrated History of China* (3rd ed.), Chapters 1–4 covers everything from prehistory through the Han.

---

## Stage 1: Foundation (2–3 months)

**Goal**: Understand the full arc of imperial Chinese history; build a mental chronological framework; master the key concepts and the major dynasties.

### Required Readings

1. **Patricia Buckley Ebrey**, *The Cambridge Illustrated History of China* (3rd ed., 2022)
   - Read the full book, cover to cover. This is your backbone.
   - Focus especially on Chapters 5–14 (covering 220–1912 CE)
   - Pay attention to the illustrations — they are pedagogically essential, not decorative

2. **Jonathan Spence**, *The Search for Modern China* (3rd ed., 2013)
   - Read Part One ("The Late Ming and Early Qing") and Part Two ("The High Qing")
   - This covers roughly 1600–1800 with more depth than Ebrey alone

### Key Concepts to Master at this Stage
- The Mandate of Heaven and the dynastic cycle
- The civil service examination system (*keju*)
- Confucianism as state ideology
- The basic geography of the dynasties (where each ruled, where the capitals were)
- The China-steppe relationship

### Supplement: Free Online Resources
- **Asia for Educators (Columbia University)**: https://afe.easia.columbia.edu — Excellent free materials organized by dynasty; good primary source excerpts
- **Asia Society Education**: https://asiasociety.org/education — Accessible overviews of each dynasty
- **World History Encyclopedia** entries on each dynasty: well-sourced, free, reliable

## Stage 2: The Early Imperial Period (2–3 months)

**Goal**: Develop deep understanding of the Period of Disunity through the Tang Dynasty; understand the role of Buddhism; appreciate the Tang as a cultural golden age.

### Chronological Focus
- Three Kingdoms, Jin Dynasty, Northern & Southern Dynasties (220–589)
- Sui Dynasty (581–618)
- Tang Dynasty (618–907)

### Required Readings

1. **Primary source**: Read excerpts from *Records of the Three Kingdoms* (Chen Shou) in translation — available in partial translation; Moss Roberts's translation of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* (4 vols., University of California Press, 1991) is excellent
   - Note clearly the distinction between the novel and the history
   - Focus on the novel's treatment of Cao Cao (a villain to Liu Bei loyalists but an admired statesman in other traditions), Zhuge Liang (the ideal advisor), and the theme of loyalty

2. **For the Tang**: Read Arthur Waley's translations of Tang poetry:
   - *170 Chinese Poems* (1918; widely available online) — includes Li Bai, Du Fu, Wang Wei, and Bai Juyi; accessible, beautiful translations
   - Alternative: David Hinton's more recent translations of *Mountain Home* (2005)

3. **For Buddhism**: Read "Buddhism in China" in the *Encyclopedia of Buddhism* online at encyclopediaofbuddhism.org — free, authoritative

4. **For the Sui**: The World History Encyclopedia article on the Sui Dynasty is an excellent, free summary: https://www.worldhistory.org/Sui_Dynasty/

### Key Figures to Know
- **Cao Cao** (155–220): Founder of the Wei state; military genius; often portrayed as villain in the novel but admired by many historians
- **Liu Bei** (161–223): Claimant to Han legitimacy; ruler of Shu Han
- **Zhuge Liang** (181–234): Liu Bei's strategist; the archetype of the wise advisor in Chinese culture
- **Emperor Wen of Sui** (541–604): Reunified China after 360 years of division; established the equal-field system and began the Grand Canal
- **Emperor Yang of Sui** (569–618): Built the Grand Canal; launched disastrous Korea campaigns; exemplar of overreaching ruler; assassinated 618
- **Emperor Taizong of Tang** (599–649): Li Shimin; widely regarded as one of China's greatest emperors; the "Zhenguan era" under him is idealized as a golden age of governance
- **Wu Zetian** (624–705): China's only female emperor; reigned de facto 660–705; female emperor 690–705; reformed examinations, patronized Buddhism, expanded Tang territory
- **Li Bai / Li Po** (701–762): "Immortal Poet"; Daoist, romantic, lyrical; ~1,000 surviving poems
- **Du Fu** (712–770): "Poet-Sage"; Confucian, socially conscious; ~1,500 surviving poems; considered by many the greatest Chinese poet
- **Wang Wei** (701–761): "Poet-Painter / Poet-Buddha"; Buddhist contemplative verse; founder of monochrome landscape painting

### Key Events
- **Battle of Red Cliffs** (208 CE): Allied Shu-Wu forces defeat Cao Cao's fleet; prevents reunification
- **Jin unification** (280 CE): Brief reunification under Jin Dynasty; collapses 316 CE under nomadic invasion
- **Grand Canal construction** (605–610 CE): Connected Yellow River to Yangtze; conscripted ~5 million workers; became the economic spine of China for 1,400 years
- **An Lushan Rebellion** (755–763 CE): The Tang's defining crisis; a general of mixed Sogdian-Turkic descent rebels; estimated 13–36 million dead; Chang'an captured twice; Tang never recovers its earlier power

## Stage 3: The Song Dynasty and the "First Great Divergence" (2 months)

**Goal**: Understand the Song's remarkable achievements and contradictions — the most economically and technologically advanced society of its era, yet militarily unable to defend itself.

### Required Readings

1. **Dieter Kuhn**, *The Age of Confucian Rule: The Song Transformation of China* (2009, Harvard University Press)
   - The best single-volume survey of the Song
   - Covers the economic revolution, technology, Neo-Confucianism, and art

2. **Primary source**: Read excerpts from Shen Kuo's *Dream Pool Essays* (*Mengxi Bitan*, 1088) — the entry on the compass, the entry on movable type, and the geological observations
   - Partial translations available in Joseph Needham's *Science and Civilisation in China* and in anthologies

3. **For Wang Anshi**: Read the Wikipedia article on "New Policies (Song dynasty)" as a starting point, then follow up with sections in Kuhn or Ebrey

### Key Technologies to Understand
- **Movable type printing** (Bi Sheng, c. 1040): First described by Shen Kuo; 400 years before Gutenberg
- **Magnetic compass** (navigation use described c. 1119): Enabled reliable open-ocean navigation; transmitted to Europe where it enabled the Age of Exploration
- **Gunpowder weapons** (fire arrows, bombs, early guns): Song China developed the first battlefield firearms to defend against Jurchen and Mongol invasions
- **Paper money** (*jiaozi*, first issued c. 960 in Sichuan): The world's first government-issued paper currency

### Key Political Arc
- 960: Founding of Northern Song by Zhao Kuangyin (Emperor Taizu)
- 1069–1076: Wang Anshi's New Policies reform program; fierce debate with conservatives (Sima Guang, Su Shi)
- 1127: **Jingkang Incident** — Jurchen Jin forces sack Kaifeng, capture Emperor Huizong and his court; beginning of the Southern Song
- 1127–1279: Southern Song, capital at Hangzhou (Lin'an); economic and cultural flourishing continues despite loss of the north
- 1279: Final Mongol conquest; last Song loyalists defeated at the naval Battle of Yamen

## Stage 4: The Mongol Interlude (1 month)

**Goal**: Understand Yuan Dynasty governance, its ethnic hierarchy, and the cultural exchange it enabled.

### Required Readings

1. **Morris Rossabi**, *Khubilai Khan: His Life and Times* (1988, University of California Press)
   - Standard biography; accessible; explains governance structures

2. **Primary source**: Marco Polo, *The Travels of Marco Polo* — read in a good translation (Ronald Latham's Penguin Classics edition is recommended)
   - Read critically: note what he notices, what he misses, and where his claims have been corroborated or questioned

### Key Concepts
- The four-class ethnic hierarchy: Mongols → Semu (Central Asian allies) → Northern Chinese → Southern Chinese
- Why the Yuan lasted only 97 years compared to the 276 years of the Ming
- How Mongol rule affected Chinese identity: increased Han Chinese consciousness, fueled the Red Turban Rebellion
- The Yuan as conduit for global exchange: Chinese gunpowder and printing westward; Persian astronomical instruments and Islamic medical knowledge eastward

---

## Stage 5: The Ming Dynasty (2 months)

**Goal**: Understand the Ming as both China's greatest restoration of Han rule and the beginning of the long-term decline vis-à-vis Europe.

### Required Readings

1. **Ray Huang (黃仁宇)**, *1587, A Year of No Significance* (1981, Yale University Press)
   - Essential; structurally innovative; uses a single year as a lens on the Ming system's structural failures
   - Shows how the Confucian bureaucratic system made reform impossible

2. **Timothy Brook**, *The Confusions of Pleasure: Commerce and Culture in Ming China* (1998, University of California Press)
   - Excellent social and economic history; the best way to understand everyday Ming life

3. **Primary source novel**: Wu Cheng'en, *Journey to the West* (Xiyouji)
   - Read Anthony Yu's 4-volume scholarly translation (University of Chicago Press), or W.J.F. Jenner's more accessible 3-volume version (Foreign Languages Press)
   - Alternatively: read the popular abridged versions first, then the full text

### Key Figures and Events
- **Hongwu Emperor / Zhu Yuanzhang** (r. 1368–1398): Orphan, monk, rebel leader, emperor; the peasant-to-emperor story; brutal purges; pro-peasant agrarian policies; abolished the chancellorship
- **Yongle Emperor** (r. 1402–1424): Usurped the throne from his nephew; built the Forbidden City (1406–1420) and moved the capital to Beijing; sponsored Zheng He's seven voyages; expanded the Great Wall
- **Zheng He** (1371–1433/35): Muslim eunuch admiral; led seven voyages (1405–1433) with fleets of ~300 ships and 28,000 sailors to Southeast Asia, India, Arabia, and East Africa; the largest naval expeditions in history before the modern era
- **Wang Yangming** (1472–1529): Neo-Confucian philosopher; developed the theory of *liangzhi* (innate moral knowledge); the "unity of knowledge and action"
- **Li Zicheng** (1606–1645): Peasant rebel who captured Beijing in 1644; last Ming emperor hanged himself; Wu Sangui let in the Manchu forces

### The Maritime Question
The abandonment of Zheng He's voyages (1433) vs. the beginning of European oceanic expansion (~1490) is one of the great counterfactual questions in world history. The conventional explanation:
- Confucian officials viewed the expeditions as wasteful prestige projects draining the treasury
- The primary security threat was land-based (the Mongols in the north), not maritime
- By 1500, China had banned construction of ocean-going ships; by 1525, ordered destruction of existing ones

## Stage 6: The Qing Dynasty (3 months)

**Goal**: Understand the Qing as both China's last and arguably greatest dynasty, and as the dynasty whose decline directly shaped modern China.

### Required Readings

1. **Jonathan Spence**, *The Search for Modern China* (3rd ed.) — complete the book; Parts Three through Five cover the Qing decline, the 19th-century crises, and the 1911 Revolution

2. **Stephen R. Platt**, *Autumn in the Heavenly Kingdom* (2012, Knopf)
   - Narrative history of the Taiping Rebellion; the best entry point to the 19th-century Qing

3. **Julia Lovell**, *The Opium War* (2011, Picador)
   - Integrates both Chinese and British perspectives; dispels myths on both sides; authoritative

4. **Primary source novel**: Cao Xueqin, *Dream of the Red Chamber* (*Honglou Meng*)
   - Read David Hawkes and John Minford's 5-volume Penguin Classics translation (*The Story of the Stone*), widely considered the finest English translation of any Chinese text
   - This is a long-term project; read at least the first volume (Chapters 1–26) to experience the Qing aristocratic world at its peak

### Key Phases of the Qing

**Phase 1: Conquest and Consolidation (1644–1683)**
- Manchu forces enter through the Shanhai Pass, invited by Ming general Wu Sangui
- The "Queue Order" (1644): all Han men required to shave the front of their head and wear a queue as a sign of submission; enforced by death ("lose your hair or lose your head")
- Resistance in the south continues through the 1670s; Taiwan held by Koxinga (Zheng Chenggong) until 1683

**Phase 2: The High Qing (1683–1799)**
- The reigns of Kangxi (r. 1661–1722), Yongzheng (r. 1722–1735), and Qianlong (r. 1735–1796) — 134 years of relative peace and prosperity
- Territorial expansion: Tibet, Xinjiang, Mongolia, Taiwan; the empire reaches 13 million km²
- Population explosion: ~150 million (1700) → ~300 million (1800) → ~450 million (1850)
- Cultural control: the literary inquisition (*wenzi yu*); Qianlong's book burning campaign (1772–1793); the *Siku Quanshu* (Complete Library of the Four Treasuries), the world's largest encyclopedia, compiled simultaneously
- The Macartney Mission (1793): Britain's failed attempt to open trade; Macartney's refusal to kowtow; Qianlong's famous dismissal of British trade goods as having "no use for your country's manufactures"

**Phase 3: The Century of Humiliation (1839–1912)**
- First Opium War (1839–1842): Britain forces China to permit opium trade; Treaty of Nanking cedes Hong Kong and opens five treaty ports
- Second Opium War (1856–1860): Britain and France; Summer Palace burned; Beijing occupied
- Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864): Hong Xiuquan, a Hakka Christian who believed himself the younger brother of Jesus, leads an uprising that controls much of southern China; estimated 20–30 million dead; the deadliest civil war in human history
- Self-Strengthening Movement (1861–1895): Li Hongzhang and others build arsenals, shipyards, and railways; limited success; exposed as inadequate by China's defeat to Japan in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95)
- Hundred Days' Reform (1898): Emperor Guangxu's attempted modernization; Empress Dowager Cixi's coup reverses all reforms
- Boxer Rebellion (1899–1901): Anti-foreign, anti-Christian uprising; Cixi backs the Boxers; Eight-Nation Alliance occupies Beijing; Boxer Protocol imposes 450 million taels indemnity
- 1911 Revolution (Xinhai Revolution): Military uprising in Wuchang (October 10, 1911); Sun Yat-sen becomes provisional president; the last emperor Puyi abdicates on February 12, 1912

## Stage 7: Cross-Cutting Themes (Ongoing, weave in throughout)

### The Needham Question
- Start with Justin Yifu Lin's article (1995) in *Economic Development and Cultural Change*
- Then read the relevant sections of Needham's *Science and Civilisation in China*, Volume 1 ("Introductory Orientations") for Needham's own framing
- Then engage with the debate: Robert Allen's *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective* (2009) for the "high wages" explanation; Kenneth Pomeranz's *The Great Divergence: China, Europe, and the Making of the Modern World Economy* (2000, Princeton University Press) for the ecological/geographic explanation

### Chinese Art and Literature
- For Tang poetry: Stephen Owen's anthology *An Anthology of Chinese Literature* (1996, Norton) contains excellent translations with scholarly apparatus
- For Song landscape painting: Visit museum collections online (the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Freer Gallery have major holdings); read Ebrey's *Cambridge Illustrated History* chapters on Song art
- For Ming novels: See Stage 5 above; also read *Water Margin* (translated as *Outlaws of the Marsh* by Sidney Shapiro, or *The Marshes of Mount Liang* by Johannes Derk Blöte)

## Recommended Sequence Summary

| Stage | Duration | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Prerequisites | 3–4 weeks | Pre-Han and Han China basics |
| Stage 1: Foundation | 2–3 months | Full arc overview; key concepts |
| Stage 2: Disunity & Tang | 2–3 months | Buddhism; cultural golden age; An Lushan |
| Stage 3: Song | 2 months | Economic revolution; Neo-Confucianism |
| Stage 4: Yuan | 1 month | Mongol rule; ethnic hierarchy |
| Stage 5: Ming | 2 months | Restoration; Zheng He; maritime question |
| Stage 6: Qing | 3 months | High Qing peak; 19th-century collapse |
| Stage 7: Cross-cutting | Ongoing | Needham; art; steppe; Great Divergence |

**Total**: 12–18 months for thorough coverage
