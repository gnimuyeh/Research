# Foundational Works: Imperial Chinese History

This document lists verified scholarly works — books, articles, and primary sources — that form the essential reading corpus for imperial Chinese history from 220 to 1912 CE. All citations have been cross-referenced.

---

## I. Essential Textbooks and Reference Works

### 1. *The Cambridge History of China* (15 volumes)
- **Editors**: Denis Twitchett, John K. Fairbank, and subsequent editors
- **Publisher**: Cambridge University Press, 1978–present
- **Coverage**: Volumes cover the full imperial period from the Qin/Han through the Qing
- **Key volumes for this period**:
  - Vol. 3: *Sui and T'ang China, 589–906* (ed. Twitchett)
  - Vol. 5: *The Sung Dynasty and Its Precursors*
  - Vol. 6: *Alien Regimes and Border States, 907–1368*
  - Vols. 7–8: *The Ming Dynasty, 1368–1644*
  - Vols. 9–15: *The Ch'ing Empire*
- **Why it matters**: The largest and most comprehensive history of China in English; contributes original research by specialist sinologists for every period; the definitive academic reference.

### 2. *The Cambridge Illustrated History of China* (3rd ed.)
- **Author**: Patricia Buckley Ebrey
- **Publisher**: Cambridge University Press, 2022 (original 1996)
- **Why it matters**: The leading single-volume textbook for Chinese history; praised as "the best concise account of the long history of Chinese culture." Endorsed by Jonathan Spence. Updated with new archaeological findings and environmental history. Essential first purchase for any serious learner.

### 3. *The Search for Modern China* (3rd ed.)
- **Author**: Jonathan D. Spence
- **Publisher**: W.W. Norton, 2013 (original 1990)
- **Why it matters**: The classic textbook for late imperial and modern China, from the late Ming (~1600) through the late 20th century. Emphasizes demographics, economics, and social history. Widely used in university courses worldwide. Spence (1936–2021) taught Chinese history at Yale for decades.

### 4. *Chinese Civilization: A Sourcebook* (2nd ed.)
- **Author**: Patricia Buckley Ebrey
- **Publisher**: Free Press, 1993
- **Why it matters**: Primary source reader covering the full span of Chinese history; essential for understanding how Chinese people actually wrote about their own world.

### 5. *Science and Civilisation in China* (7 volumes, 27 books)
- **Author**: Joseph Needham (with collaborators)
- **Publisher**: Cambridge University Press, 1954–present
- **Why it matters**: Needham's monumental, life's-work documentation of Chinese science and technology. Fundamentally overturned Eurocentric historiography by demonstrating China's technological lead in paper, printing, compass, gunpowder, cast iron, and dozens of other fields. Raised the "Needham Question" (why China did not industrialize first) which remains one of the central debates of economic history. Described as "perhaps the greatest single act of historical synthesis and intercultural communication ever attempted by one man."

### 6. *Routledge Handbook of Imperial Chinese History*
- **Editors**: Victor Cunrui Xiong and Kenneth Hammond
- **Publisher**: Routledge, 2018
- **Why it matters**: Covers the entire imperial period (221 BCE–1912 CE); treats politics and dynastic transitions as driving forces of change; comprehensive academic reference for advanced students.

## II. Key Works by Dynasty/Period

### Period of Disunity / Six Dynasties (220–589)

#### 7. *Records of the Three Kingdoms* (三国志, *Sanguozhi*)
- **Author**: Chen Shou (233–297 CE)
- **Date**: Late 3rd century CE; supplemented by Pei Songzhi's annotations (429 CE)
- **Why it matters**: The authoritative primary historical source for the Three Kingdoms period (220–280 CE). Chen Shou was a Jin dynasty official who compiled official histories of Wei, Shu, and Wu into a single work. Pei Songzhi's annotations nearly doubled the size by adding supplementary texts. Everything in *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* (the novel) derives ultimately from this source.

#### 8. *Romance of the Three Kingdoms* (三国演义, *Sanguozhi Yanyi*)
- **Author**: Attributed to Luo Guanzhong (c. 1330–1400)
- **Date**: 14th century; set during 184–280 CE
- **Why it matters**: One of China's Four Classic Novels; among the most widely read historical novels in Chinese history; shaped popular understanding of the Three Kingdoms era for 600 years. Blends history with dramatization — it is roughly 70% historical fact and 30% fiction. The basis for understanding Chinese popular culture, strategic idioms, and political mythology.

### Tang Dynasty (618–907)

#### 9. *Complete Tang Poems* (全唐诗, *Quan Tangshi*)
- **Compiled by**: The Qing imperial court (1705)
- **Contents**: Over 48,900 poems by more than 2,200 Tang-era authors
- **Why it matters**: The definitive anthology of Tang poetry; contains the complete surviving output of Li Bai, Du Fu, Wang Wei, Bai Juyi, and all major Tang poets. The primary source for Tang literary and cultural history.

#### 10. Key Scholarship on the Tang
- **Denis Twitchett**, ed., *The Cambridge History of China, Vol. 3: Sui and T'ang China, 589–906* (1979) — Essential scholarly reference
- **Charles Benn**, *China's Golden Age: Everyday Life in the Tang Dynasty* (2002, Oxford University Press) — Accessible account of Tang social life and culture

### Song Dynasty (960–1279)

#### 11. *Dream Pool Essays* (梦溪笔谈, *Mengxi Bitan*)
- **Author**: Shen Kuo (Shen Gua, 1031–1095)
- **Date**: 1088 CE
- **Why it matters**: The greatest scientific encyclopedia of the pre-modern Chinese world. Contains the first written description of the magnetic compass and its declination from true north; the first description of movable type printing (invented by Bi Sheng); observations on geology, astronomy, mathematics, and technology that were centuries ahead of their European equivalents. Joseph Needham called Shen Kuo "one of the greatest scientific minds in Chinese history."

#### 12. Key Scholarship on the Song
- **Robert Hartwell**, "Markets, Technology, and the Structure of Enterprise in the Development of the Eleventh-Century Chinese Iron and Steel Industry," *Journal of Economic History* (1966) — Landmark article establishing the proto-industrial nature of Song iron production
- **Robert M. Hartwell** and the broader scholarship on the Song "Economic Revolution" remain foundational
- **Dieter Kuhn**, *The Age of Confucian Rule: The Song Transformation of China* (2009, Harvard University Press) — Recommended comprehensive survey of the Song

### Yuan Dynasty (1271–1368)

#### 13. *Travels of Marco Polo* (*Il Milione*)
- **Author**: Marco Polo (1254–1324), dictated to Rustichello da Pisa
- **Date**: c. 1300
- **Why it matters**: The most influential European eyewitness account of Yuan China; describes Kublai Khan's court, the postal relay system, paper money, coal, and the cities of China. Contested by some scholars but substantially corroborated by Chinese and Persian sources.

#### 14. Key Scholarship on the Yuan
- **Morris Rossabi**, *Khubilai Khan: His Life and Times* (1988, University of California Press) — The standard English-language biography of Kublai Khan
- **Timothy Brook**, *The Troubled Empire: China in the Yuan and Ming Dynasties* (2010, Harvard University Press) — Excellent coverage of the environmental and social history of the Yuan-Ming transition

### Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)

#### 15. *Journey to the West* (西游记, *Xiyouji*)
- **Author**: Wu Cheng'en (c. 1500–1582)
- **Date**: Published 1592
- **Why it matters**: One of China's Four Classic Novels; the most internationally recognized work of Chinese fiction; a Buddhist-Daoist-Confucian allegory wrapped in comic adventure. The novel crystallized Ming-era popular religious culture and continues to generate adaptations worldwide.

#### 16. *Water Margin* (水浒传, *Shuihu Zhuan*)
- **Author**: Attributed to Shi Nai'an (fl. 14th century)
- **Date**: 14th century; set in Northern Song period
- **Why it matters**: One of China's Four Classic Novels; a foundational text of Chinese popular culture; banned in the Ming for its potential to inspire rebellion. The archetypical Chinese bandit-hero narrative.

#### 17. Key Scholarship on the Ming
- **Frederick W. Mote** and **Denis Twitchett**, eds., *The Cambridge History of China, Vols. 7–8: The Ming Dynasty* (1988, 1998) — The authoritative academic reference
- **Ray Huang (黃仁宇)**, *1587, A Year of No Significance: The Ming Dynasty in Decline* (1981, Yale University Press) — Landmark work showing the structural failures of the late Ming fiscal system; an essential and accessible read
- **Timothy Brook**, *The Confusions of Pleasure: Commerce and Culture in Ming China* (1998, University of California Press) — Excellent social and economic history

### Qing Dynasty (1644–1912)

#### 18. *Dream of the Red Chamber* (红楼梦, *Honglou Meng*)
- **Author**: Cao Xueqin (c. 1715–1763)
- **Date**: c. 1760; published posthumously
- **Why it matters**: Widely considered the greatest novel in Chinese literary history; set in a declining aristocratic family under the High Qing; a detailed portrait of 18th-century gentry life, gender relations, Buddhism and Daoism, and dynastic decline. The field of "Redology" (*honglou meng xue*) is devoted entirely to its study.

#### 19. Key Scholarship on the Qing
- **Jonathan Spence**, *The Search for Modern China* (1990) — Essential starting point for Qing through modern China
- **Jonathan Spence**, *Treason by the Book* (2001, Viking) — Fascinating account of the Yongzheng Emperor's literary inquisition campaign; illuminates Qing political culture
- **Mark C. Elliott**, *The Manchu Way: The Eight Banners and Ethnic Identity in Late Imperial China* (2001, Stanford University Press) — Foundational "New Qing History" work arguing for the persistence of Manchu ethnic identity
- **Pamela Kyle Crossley**, *A Translucent Mirror: History and Identity in Qing Imperial Ideology* (1999) — Key New Qing History work
- **Stephen R. Platt**, *Autumn in the Heavenly Kingdom: China, the West, and the Epic Story of the Taiping Civil War* (2012, Knopf) — Best narrative account of the Taiping Rebellion for general readers; well-researched
- **Julia Lovell**, *The Opium War: Drugs, Dreams and the Making of China* (2011, Picador) — Definitive account integrating both British and Chinese perspectives on the Opium Wars

## III. Cross-Cutting Scholarly Works

#### 20. Justin Yifu Lin, "The Needham Puzzle: Why the Industrial Revolution Did Not Originate in China"
- **Journal**: *Economic Development and Cultural Change*, Vol. 43, No. 2 (January 1995), pp. 269–292
- **Publisher**: University of Chicago Press
- **Why it matters**: The most cited academic article engaging with the Needham Question; argues that China's examination-based system favored experience-based rather than experiment-based technological change, contributing to the failure to develop modern science.

#### 21. Thomas Barfield, *The Perilous Frontier: Nomadic Empires and China, 221 BC to AD 1757*
- **Publisher**: Blackwell, 1989
- **Why it matters**: Proposed the influential "shadow empire" thesis — that steppe nomadic confederacies (Xiongnu, Turks, Mongols) rose and fell in tandem with Chinese dynasties, living off extraction of Chinese wealth. A key theoretical framework for understanding China-steppe relations.

#### 22. Ping-ti Ho, "In Defense of Sinicization: A Rebuttal of Evelyn Rawski's 1996 AAS Presidential Address"
- **Journal**: *Journal of Asian Studies*, Vol. 57, No. 1 (1998)
- **Why it matters**: A central document in the "New Qing History" debate; Ho argued that sinicization — not Manchu ethnic distinctiveness — was the key to Qing success, directly challenging the New Qing History school.
