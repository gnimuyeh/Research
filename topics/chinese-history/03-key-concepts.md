# Key Concepts in Chinese History — Organized by Period

## Level 1: Foundational Concepts (Prerequisites)

### 1. The Mandate of Heaven (天命, Tianming)
The concept that Heaven (Tian) grants legitimacy to a virtuous ruler and withdraws it from a corrupt one. Originated with the Zhou Dynasty's justification for overthrowing the Shang (~1046 BCE). Unlike the European "divine right of kings," the Mandate could be revoked — making revolution theoretically legitimate. Natural disasters, famines, and rebellions were interpreted as signs of withdrawal. This is the foundational political concept in Chinese history — every dynasty claimed it, every fall was explained by its loss.

### 2. The Dynastic Cycle
The recurring pattern: founding vigor → stability/prosperity → corruption/overextension → natural disasters/rebellion → fall → new dynasty. Useful as a framework but debated by historians — some see it as a genuine pattern, others as a narrative device imposed after the fact. Environmental factors (climate, floods, droughts) and demographic pressures often correlated with dynastic falls.

### 3. Confucianism (儒家, Rujia)
The philosophical tradition founded by Confucius (551-479 BCE) that became the dominant ideology of Chinese civilization for over 2,000 years. Core concepts:
- **Ren (仁)**: Humaneness, benevolence — the highest virtue
- **Li (礼)**: Ritual propriety, social norms, proper conduct
- **Xiao (孝)**: Filial piety — devotion to parents and ancestors
- **Junzi (君子)**: The "gentleman" or "exemplary person"
- **Five Relationships (五伦)**: Ruler-subject, father-son, husband-wife, elder-younger brother, friend-friend
- **Zhengming (正名)**: Rectification of names — things should match their designated roles

### 4. Daoism (道家/道教)
Two related but distinct traditions:
- **Philosophical Daoism** (Daojia): Laozi's *Dao De Jing* and Zhuangzi. The Dao (Way) is the ineffable source of all things; wu wei (non-action/effortless action) is the ideal; simplicity surpasses artifice.
- **Religious Daoism** (Daojiao): Organized religious tradition with priests, temples, rituals, alchemy, and pursuit of immortality. Developed from the Han Dynasty onward.

### 5. Yin-Yang and Five Elements (阴阳五行)
- **Yin-Yang**: Complementary opposites (dark/light, cold/hot, passive/active). Not good vs. evil but interdependent forces.
- **Five Elements/Phases (Wuxing)**: Wood, fire, earth, metal, water — each generates and conquers another in cycles. Applied to governance, medicine, calendar, music, and virtually every domain of Chinese thought.
- Systematized by Zou Yan (c. 305-240 BCE), synthesized with Confucianism by Dong Zhongshu (179-104 BCE) into Han state ideology.

### 6. The Chinese Writing System
Oracle bone script (Shang, c. 1250 BCE) → Bronze inscriptions (Zhou) → Small Seal Script (Qin standardization) → Clerical Script (Han) → Regular Script (Han-Tang, modern form). The world's oldest continuously used writing system. Enabled cultural unity across China's vast territory and diverse spoken dialects — people who couldn't understand each other's speech could communicate through writing.

## Level 2: Imperial System Concepts

### 7. The Examination System (科举, Keju)
Meritocratic selection of government officials through competitive written examinations. Roots in Sui Dynasty (605 CE), expanded through Tang, became the primary path to office in Song, rigidified in Ming-Qing (the "Eight-Legged Essay" format), abolished in 1905. The longest-lasting meritocratic bureaucratic system in world history (~1,300 years). Promoted social mobility but also cultural conservatism.

### 8. The Scholar-Official Class (士大夫, Shidafu)
The educated elite who governed China through the examination system. Combined literary, artistic, philosophical, and administrative functions. Reached their cultural peak in the Song Dynasty. Their values — Confucian morality, literary cultivation, public service — defined Chinese elite culture for a millennium.

### 9. Fengjian vs. Junxian (封建 vs. 郡县)
Two competing models of governance:
- **Fengjian** (feudal enfeoffment): Zhou-era system of granting territories to relatives/allies. Decentralized.
- **Junxian** (commanderies and counties): Qin innovation of centralized bureaucratic administration with appointed officials. This became the template for Chinese governance for 2,000 years.

### 10. Legalism (法家, Fajia)
The political philosophy that built the Qin Empire. Key thinkers: Shang Yang, Han Feizi, Li Si. Three tools of governance: **fa** (law — clear, public, impartial), **shu** (techniques — keeping officials in check), **shi** (positional power of the ruler). Harsh but effective. The Qin/Han synthesis combined Legalist administrative methods with Confucian ideological legitimacy — a pattern that persisted.

### 11. Neo-Confucianism (理学/心学)
The revival and reformulation of Confucianism in the Song Dynasty, incorporating Buddhist and Daoist metaphysical ideas:
- **Zhu Xi (1130-1200)**: The dominant thinker. Emphasized **li** (理, principle) and **qi** (气, material force). His commentaries on the Four Books became the examination standard from 1313-1905.
- **Wang Yangming (1472-1529)**: Rival school. Argued knowledge and action are inseparable ("unity of knowledge and action"), and moral knowledge is innate.

### 12. The Tributary System (朝贡体系)
China's traditional framework for foreign relations. Surrounding states (Korea, Vietnam, Ryukyu, etc.) sent tribute missions to the Chinese emperor, performing ritual submission. In return, they received legitimacy, trade access, and gifts often exceeding the tribute's value. Not colonialism — more a hierarchical diplomatic order centered on Chinese cultural superiority.

## Level 3: Transformation and Crisis Concepts

### 13. The Silk Road(s)
Trade and cultural exchange networks connecting China to Central Asia, the Middle East, and ultimately Europe. Opened by Zhang Qian's missions (138 BCE) under Emperor Wu of Han. Not a single road but a network of land and maritime routes. Transmitted goods (silk, spices, horses), technologies (papermaking, gunpowder), religions (Buddhism, Islam, Nestorianism), and diseases. The term was coined by Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877.

### 14. Buddhism in China
Entered via the Silk Road during the Han Dynasty. Flourished during the Period of Disunity (220-589 CE) when political chaos made its message of impermanence appealing. Key developments: Chinese schools emerged (Pure Land, Chan/Zen), massive cave temple construction (Mogao, Yungang, Longmen), integration with Confucianism and Daoism into the "Three Teachings" synthesis. Periodically suppressed (845 CE persecution under Tang) but permanently woven into Chinese culture.

### 15. China and the Steppe
The defining geopolitical tension of imperial China. Key antagonists across history: Xiongnu → Xianbei → Turks → Khitans → Jurchens → Mongols → Manchus. The Great Wall is its physical manifestation. Pattern: northern peoples conquer China → adopt Chinese governance → become sinicized → are eventually replaced by the next nomadic power. Recent scholarship (Barfield, Di Cosmo) emphasizes this was a complex, co-dependent relationship, not simply "barbarians vs. civilization."

### 16. The "Century of Humiliation" (百年国耻, 1839-1949)
The narrative arc from the First Opium War to the founding of the PRC. The Opium Wars, unequal treaties, territorial concessions (Hong Kong, Taiwan), the Taiping Rebellion, the Boxer Rebellion, Japanese invasion, and civil war. This historical memory is central to modern Chinese nationalism and CCP legitimacy. Drives sensitivity around sovereignty issues (Taiwan, Hong Kong, Tibet, South China Sea). "Never again" is the emotional core of Chinese foreign policy.

### 17. The Opium Wars and Unequal Treaties
- **First Opium War (1839-1842)**: Britain forced open China's markets after Commissioner Lin Zexu destroyed opium stocks. Treaty of Nanjing ceded Hong Kong, opened treaty ports, imposed indemnities.
- **Second Opium War (1856-1860)**: Anglo-French forces occupied Beijing, burned the Old Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan).
- **Unequal treaties**: Imposed extraterritoriality (foreigners immune to Chinese law), fixed tariffs, most-favored-nation clauses, and territorial concessions. Not fully abolished until 1943.

### 18. The Great Divergence
Why didn't China — which led the world in technology and economy for most of history — industrialize first? Key arguments:
- **Pomeranz**: China and Europe were comparable until ~1750; divergence caused by European access to New World resources and coal geography.
- **Elvin's "High-Level Equilibrium Trap"**: China's pre-industrial economy was so efficient that incentive to mechanize was insufficient.
- **Other factors**: geographic fragmentation of Europe promoting competition, Confucian attitudes toward commerce (debated), lack of patent protections.

## Level 4: Modern and Contemporary Concepts

### 19. Sun Yat-sen's Three Principles (三民主义)
The ideological foundation of Republican China:
1. **Nationalism** (民族主义): End foreign domination, unite China's peoples
2. **Democracy** (民权主义): Popular sovereignty through constitutional government
3. **People's Livelihood** (民生主义): Economic equality
Claimed by both the KMT (Taiwan) and CCP (mainland) as ideological heritage.

### 20. May Fourth Movement (1919)
Student-led protests over Versailles Treaty betrayal evolved into a broad intellectual revolution: attack on traditional Confucian culture, adoption of vernacular Chinese, embrace of "Mr. Science and Mr. Democracy." The intellectual wellspring of both Chinese liberalism and communism. Key figures: Chen Duxiu, Hu Shi, Lu Xun.

### 21. Maoism and Mass Campaigns
Mao Zedong adapted Marxism-Leninism to Chinese conditions — peasant revolution rather than proletarian, mass mobilization as the primary tool of transformation:
- **Land Reform** (1949-1953): 1-2 million landlords killed
- **Hundred Flowers/Anti-Rightist** (1956-1958): ~550,000 intellectuals labeled rightists
- **Great Leap Forward** (1958-1962): Attempted rapid industrialization caused the worst famine in human history (30-45 million dead)
- **Cultural Revolution** (1966-1976): Purge of "capitalist roaders," destruction of cultural heritage, sent-down youth. 500,000-2 million killed.

### 22. Reform and Opening Up (改革开放)
Deng Xiaoping's transformation beginning 1978. Key elements: Household Responsibility System (agriculture), Special Economic Zones (Shenzhen), Township and Village Enterprises, "socialist market economy." Result: ~10% annual GDP growth for three decades, 800+ million lifted from poverty, the largest economic transformation in human history. Deng's pragmatism: "It doesn't matter whether a cat is black or white; if it catches mice, it's a good cat."

### 23. The CCP's Grand Bargain (Post-Tiananmen)
After the 1989 Tiananmen Square crackdown, the implicit social contract: the CCP delivers prosperity in exchange for unchallenged political power. Economic reform continued; political reform halted. This bargain defined China from 1989 to the present, though Xi Jinping's centralization of power has modified the equation.

### 24. The Taiwan Question
Taiwan was ceded to Japan (1895-1945), governed by ROC after 1945, became the KMT's refuge after 1949. Both PRC and ROC claimed to be the legitimate government of all China. Taiwan democratized in the 1990s and has developed a distinct identity. The PRC has never renounced force for reunification. The US maintains "strategic ambiguity." One of the world's most dangerous flashpoints.

### 25. Xi Jinping's New Era
Since 2012: anti-corruption campaign (1.5M+ officials disciplined), Belt and Road Initiative (140+ countries), abolition of term limits (2018), Hong Kong National Security Law (2020), Xinjiang policies, "Chinese Dream" and "great rejuvenation." The most centralized Chinese leadership since Mao.
