# The Story of Dating Apps — From Newspaper Ads to AI Matchmakers

## Chapter 1: Before the Internet (1959–1994)

### Computers Have Been Matchmaking Since the 1950s

The idea of using technology to find love is older than you'd think:

- **1959:** Two Stanford students created the **Happy Families Planning Service** — they used an IBM 650 computer and punch card questionnaires to match 49 men with 49 women. The first computerized matchmaking.
- **1965:** Harvard students Jeff Tarr and Vaughan Morrill launched **Operation Match**, using surveys and an IBM 1401 to pair thousands of daters. Cost: $3 each.
- **1980s–90s:** Video dating services emerged — you'd record a VHS tape of yourself talking to camera, and other members would watch the tapes to decide if they wanted to meet.
- **Newspaper personal ads** were the dominant way to meet strangers for decades: "SWF, 28, loves hiking and jazz, seeking kind SWM for friendship and possibly more."

The pattern is clear: every generation has used whatever technology is available to solve the same ancient problem — finding a compatible partner.

## Chapter 2: The Web Era (1995–2011)

### Match.com — The Pioneer (1995)

**Gary Kremen**, a Stanford MBA, founded Match.com in 1993 and launched it in April 1995 — three years before Google existed. The initial idea: bring newspaper personal ads online with photos and searchable profiles.

Kremen gave early users free lifetime memberships to build the database. The site was met with skepticism — the idea that normal people would look for love on the internet was considered desperate and weird in the 1990s.

**Ironic footnote:** Kremen's own girlfriend left him for a man she met on Match.com.

Match.com proved the market existed and was eventually acquired by IAC, becoming the seed of what would become Match Group.

### eHarmony — The "Scientific" Approach (2000)

**Dr. Neil Clark Warren**, a clinical psychologist and marriage counselor with a Ph.D. from the University of Chicago, took a radically different approach. Rather than letting people browse freely, eHarmony would:

1. Require users to fill out a **lengthy questionnaire** (originally 436 questions, later shortened)
2. Run answers through a proprietary algorithm based on **"29 Dimensions of Compatibility"** (later expanded to 32)
3. Present curated matches rather than letting users search

Warren developed his system by studying 800 married couples, identifying what distinguished the happiest 200 from the least happy 200. The key factors: emotional temperament, social style, cognitive mode, and relationship skills.

**eHarmony's legacy:** It was the first dating service to claim a scientific basis for matching. Whether the science actually worked better than random matching is still debated — but the *idea* that algorithms could predict compatibility became foundational. It's the direct ancestor of today's AI-driven matching.

### OkCupid — Data-Driven and Free (2004)

Founded by four Harvard math majors, OkCupid took yet another approach: make everything free, use data openly, and let users answer hundreds of questions to find compatible matches based on shared values.

OkCupid published a famous blog called **OkTrends** that used their data to reveal uncomfortable truths about dating: racial preferences, what makes a good profile photo, how messaging patterns differ by gender. It was one of the first data-driven analyses of human dating behavior at scale.

Acquired by Match Group in 2011 for $50 million.

## Chapter 3: The Mobile Revolution (2012–2017)

### Tinder — The Swipe That Changed Everything (2012)

**Sean Rad** and **Joe Munoz** built the prototype for Tinder during a 48-hour hackathon at Hatch Labs (an IAC-funded startup incubator) in February 2012. The original name was **MatchBox**.

Tinder introduced several innovations that transformed the industry:

1. **The swipe:** Swipe right to like, swipe left to pass. Simple, addictive, game-like. (The swipe mechanic was partly inspired by "Hot or Not" websites.)
2. **Double opt-in:** You only see someone if you both liked each other. This reduced rejection anxiety.
3. **Mobile-first:** Unlike Match.com and eHarmony (which were desktop websites), Tinder was built for phones from day one.
4. **Location-based:** Used GPS to show you people nearby, making the connection feel immediate and actionable.
5. **Minimal friction:** No long questionnaires. Just photos, a short bio, and start swiping.

Tinder was seeded on college campuses (Rad's co-founder **Whitney Wolfe Herd** drove adoption at USC, SMU, and other schools). It went viral. By 2014, it had 1 billion swipes per day.

**Key drama:** In 2014, Whitney Wolfe Herd left Tinder after filing a sexual harassment lawsuit against co-founder Justin Mateen. In 2018, Tinder co-founders including Rad filed a $2 billion lawsuit against Match Group, claiming IAC had deliberately undervalued Tinder to reduce their payouts.

### Bumble — Women Make the First Move (2014)

After leaving Tinder, **Whitney Wolfe Herd** founded Bumble with a single differentiator: in heterosexual matches, only women can send the first message. This was designed to:
- Reduce harassment (women weren't flooded with unwanted messages)
- Shift power dynamics
- Attract women who were tired of the Tinder experience

Bumble grew to 100 million users and went public in 2021, making Wolfe Herd the youngest female CEO to take a company public at age 31, with a net worth briefly exceeding $1.5 billion.

**Match Group tried to buy Bumble** for $450 million in 2017. Wolfe Herd rejected the offer. Match then sued Bumble for patent infringement (the swipe), and Bumble countersued. They eventually settled.

### Hinge — "Designed to Be Deleted" (2012, relaunched 2016)

**Justin McLeod** founded Hinge in 2012 as a connections-through-mutual-friends app. It nearly failed. In 2016, McLeod completely relaunched it with a new philosophy: **"Designed to be deleted."**

Instead of swiping through an infinite deck, Hinge showed detailed profiles and required users to like or comment on specific photos or prompts. The idea: encourage more intentional engagement, less mindless swiping.

Hinge was acquired by Match Group in 2018. By 2024, it generated **$550 million in revenue** (8.9% market share) and is the fastest-growing major dating app, with revenue growing 26% year-over-year while competitors shrank.

**In December 2025, McLeod stepped down as Hinge CEO to launch Overtone**, a new AI-powered dating service.

### Grindr — The First Location-Based Dating App (2009)

**Joel Simkhai** launched Grindr in March 2009 as a GPS-based app for gay, bi, trans, and queer men. When he heard the next iPhone would include GPS, he realized geolocation was perfect for meeting gay men nearby — a population that couldn't always identify each other in public spaces.

Grindr was actually the **first mainstream location-based dating app** — predating Tinder by three years. It proved the model that Tinder would later adapt for a broader audience.

## Chapter 4: The AI Transformation (2018–2026)

### Phase 1: AI Behind the Scenes (2018–2022)

Early AI adoption was invisible to users:
- **Better matching algorithms:** Moving from simple filter matching (age, location) to machine learning models that learn from user behavior
- **Content moderation:** AI scanning photos for nudity, spam, and policy violations
- **Fraud detection:** Machine learning identifying fake profiles and scammers
- **Photo quality assessment:** AI evaluating which photos would perform best

### Phase 2: AI Becomes the Product (2023–2024)

After ChatGPT showed what LLMs could do, dating apps rushed to integrate visible AI features:

- **Bumble's Deception Detector** (February 2024): AI that proactively identifies and blocks 95% of fake profiles before users see them. Reduced user reports of fakes by 45% in two months.
- **Tinder's Face Check** (expanded 2025): Mandatory facial verification creating a 3D "FaceMap" from video selfies. Reduced bad actor exposure by 60%. Part of a **$60 million AI investment** by Tinder.
- **Tinder's "Chemistry"** (2025): AI feature that learns about users through questions and Camera Roll photo analysis to give "a single drop" of targeted recommendations instead of mass swiping.
- **Hinge's AI recommendation engine** (late 2025): Users are 8x more likely to go on a date with their "Most Compatible" AI match.
- **Bumble 2.0** (launching Q2 2026): Complete platform rebuild. AI assistant **"Bee"** synthesizes profile data and behavior to identify one highly compatible profile at a time, with an explanation of *why* they match.

### Phase 3: AI-Native Apps (2025–2026)

A new wave of startups built around AI from the ground up:

- **Known:** No swiping at all. Users have a 26-minute voice conversation with an AI agent that learns their personality and values, then the AI arranges one match at a time. Claims 80% of introductions lead to in-person dates.

- **Sitch:** An AI matchmaker named "Hailey" interviews users with 50+ questions (text or voice), then actively makes introductions and facilitates group chats between matches. Backed by a16z and M13.

- **Overtone:** Justin McLeod's (Hinge founder) new venture — uses AI and voice tools for "more thoughtful and personal" connection. Match Group is leading the initial funding.

### The Trend Line

```
1995-2010: Questionnaires → Manual browsing → Human judgment
2012-2018: Swipe mechanics → Simple algorithmic ranking → Engagement optimization
2018-2023: ML-based matching → Behavioral learning → Content moderation AI
2023-2025: LLM integration → AI coaches → Photo/identity verification
2025-2026: Voice AI → AI matchmakers → No-swipe paradigms → Agent-based dating
```

The direction is clear: from **"here are 1,000 profiles, you figure it out"** toward **"I know you, I'll find you one great match."**
