# Ancient Chinese History: Foundational Papers, Books, and Key Figures

## Essential Academic Works

### 1. The Cambridge History of Ancient China
**Editors:** Michael Loewe and Edward L. Shaughnessy
**Publisher:** Cambridge University Press, 1999
**Why it matters:** The single most authoritative English-language academic reference on pre-imperial China, covering origins through 221 BCE. Includes chapters by leading specialists on archaeology, writing, philosophy, and social organization. It is the standard starting point for serious academic study.

### 2. Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian / 史記)
**Author:** Sima Qian (c. 145–86 BCE)
**Completed:** c. 91 BCE
**Why it matters:** The foundational primary source for all of ancient Chinese history. Covers 2,000+ years from the Yellow Emperor to Emperor Wu of Han in 130 chapters (~520,000 characters). Invented the biographical annals format that became the template for all subsequent official Chinese histories. Sima Qian is considered the "Father of Chinese Historiography." Despite being written in the Han period, it remains the most cited ancient source for Shang, Zhou, Qin, and early Han history. Available in English translation by Burton Watson (Columbia University Press).
**Source:** [Wikipedia — Shiji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiji), [EBSCO — Sima Qian's Chinese History](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/sima-qians-chinese-history)

### 3. The Analects (Lunyu / 論語)
**Attributed to:** Confucius (551–479 BCE), compiled by his disciples
**Final form:** c. 3rd–2nd century BCE
**Why it matters:** The primary source for Confucian thought, which became state ideology under the Han and shaped Chinese culture for 2,000+ years. Covers ethics, governance, ritual, filial piety, and the concept of ren (benevolence). The most widely studied Chinese philosophical text in history.
**Source:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — Confucius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/)

### 4. Dao De Jing (Tao Te Ching / 道德經)
**Attributed to:** Laozi (Lao-tzu)
**Date:** c. 4th–3rd century BCE (traditional attribution to 6th century BCE is contested)
**Why it matters:** Foundational text of Daoism; 81 short chapters exploring the nature of the Dao (Way), non-action (wu wei), and harmony with nature. Became the second most important philosophical tradition in Chinese history after Confucianism.
**Source:** [Hundred Schools of Thought — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Schools_of_Thought)

### 5. The Art of War (Sunzi Bingfa / 孫子兵法)
**Attributed to:** Sun Tzu (Sun Wu), traditionally c. 512 BCE
**Actual composition:** Most scholars date it to the Warring States period (c. 481–221 BCE)
**Why it matters:** The oldest surviving military treatise in the world. 13 chapters on strategy, deception, terrain, and leadership. Influenced military doctrine across East Asia and, in translation, worldwide. The 1972 Yinqueshan bamboo slip discovery confirmed its ancient origins. Authorship and dating remain scholarly debates.
**Source:** [Wikipedia — Sun Tzu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_Tzu), [World History Encyclopedia — Sun-Tzu](https://www.worldhistory.org/Sun-Tzu/)

### 6. The Book of Changes (Yijing / I Ching / 易經)
**Composition:** Core text c. 9th–8th century BCE; commentaries (Ten Wings) attributed to the Zhou period
**Why it matters:** One of the oldest Chinese texts, used for divination, cosmology, and later integrated into Confucian and Daoist philosophy. Central to the Yin-Yang and Five Elements worldview. Became one of the Five Confucian Classics foundational to the imperial examination system.

### 7. "Rethinking Erlitou: Legend, History and Chinese Archaeology"
**Author:** Lothar von Falkenhausen
**Published in:** Cambridge Archaeological Journal, 2003
**Why it matters:** A key Western scholarly intervention in the debate over whether the Erlitou site represents the Xia Dynasty. Critiques methodological nationalism in Chinese archaeology and argues the question of Xia's historicity cannot be settled by physical artifacts alone. Essential reading for understanding the Xia controversy.
**Source:** [ResearchGate — Rethinking Erlitou](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273293121_Rethinking_Erlitou_Legend_History_and_Chinese_Archaeology)

### 8. Chinese History: A New Manual
**Author:** Endymion Wilkinson
**Publisher:** Harvard University Asia Center, 2012 (4th edition)
**Why it matters:** The definitive annotated bibliography and research guide to Chinese history. Covers primary and secondary sources, databases, and historiographical methods. The standard reference for researchers.
**Source:** [University of Kansas — Ancient China Subject Guide](https://guides.lib.ku.edu/c.php?g=95017&p=617701)

### 9. Sources of Chinese Tradition (2 vols.)
**Editors:** William Theodore de Bary and Irene Bloom
**Publisher:** Columbia University Press, 1999 (2nd edition)
**Why it matters:** The standard anthology of primary sources for Chinese intellectual and cultural history, from oracle bones through the 20th century. Essential for studying the Hundred Schools of Thought.

### 10. A Companion to Chinese Archaeology
**Editor:** Anne P. Underhill
**Publisher:** Wiley-Blackwell, 2013
**Why it matters:** A comprehensive collection of essays by leading archaeologists covering all major periods and regions of ancient China. Best single volume for understanding the archaeological evidence behind ancient Chinese history.

### 11. "Outburst Flood at 1920 BCE Supports Historicity of China's Great Flood and the Xia Dynasty"
**Authors:** Wu Qinglong et al.
**Published in:** Science, vol. 353, 2016
**Why it matters:** A landmark paper using geological and archaeological evidence to identify a massive flood at Jishi Gorge around 1920 BCE. The authors propose this real catastrophe may be the origin of the Great Flood legend and the mytho-historical figure of Yu the Great, whose flood control work is said to have founded the Xia Dynasty. Generated significant scholarly debate.

### 12. The Cambridge Illustrated History of China
**Author:** Patricia Buckley Ebrey
**Publisher:** Cambridge University Press, 2nd edition, 2010
**Why it matters:** The best single-volume illustrated narrative history of China for general readers and students. Covers all major periods with integration of archaeological and textual evidence. Highly accessible.
**Source:** [Five Books — Chinese History](https://fivebooks.com/category/history-books/chinese-history/)

## Key Figures in Ancient Chinese History

### Primary Historical Figures

| Figure | Dates | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Confucius (Kong Fuzi)** | 551–479 BCE | Philosopher; founder of Confucianism; *Analects*; shaped Chinese ethics and governance for 2,000+ years |
| **Laozi** | c. 6th–4th century BCE (debated) | Attributed founder of Daoism; *Dao De Jing* |
| **Mozi (Mo Di)** | c. 470–391 BCE | Founder of Mohism; universal love, meritocracy, anti-war philosophy |
| **Sun Tzu (Sun Wu)** | Traditionally c. 544–496 BCE | Military strategist; *Art of War* (authorship debated) |
| **Shang Yang (Wei Yang)** | c. 390–338 BCE | Legalist reformer of Qin; agricultural and military reforms that enabled Qin's eventual unification |
| **Han Feizi** | c. 280–233 BCE | Systematizer of Legalism; influenced Qin Shi Huang's governance |
| **Qin Shi Huang (Ying Zheng)** | 259–210 BCE | First Emperor of unified China; founded Qin Dynasty; standardized writing, weights, measures, currency; built early Great Wall; Terracotta Army |
| **Liu Bang (Emperor Gaozu)** | 256–195 BCE | Founder of Han Dynasty; commoner who rose to emperor |
| **Emperor Wu of Han (Liu Che)** | 157–87 BCE | Greatest Han emperor; established Confucianism as state ideology; opened Silk Road; major territorial expansion |
| **Sima Qian** | c. 145–86 BCE | "Father of Chinese Historiography"; author of *Shiji* |
| **Wang Mang** | 45 BCE–23 CE | Usurped Han throne; founded short-lived Xin Dynasty; attempted radical Confucian reforms |
| **Zhang Jue** | d. 184 CE | Leader of Yellow Turban Rebellion; precipitated collapse of Eastern Han |

### Key Archaeologists and Modern Scholars

| Scholar | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Li Ji (Li Chi)** | "Father of Chinese Archaeology"; led Yinxu excavations 1928–1937; established scientific Chinese archaeology |
| **Xu Xusheng** | Led 1959 surveys identifying the Erlitou site; first to propose Erlitou as a Xia capital |
| **Dong Zhongshu** | Han-era Confucian philosopher (179–104 BCE) who synthesized Confucianism, Yin-Yang theory, and Five Elements into the Han state ideology |
| **Gu Jiegang** | Founded the "Doubting Antiquity School" in the 1920s; challenged historicity of legendary figures including the Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors |
| **Edward L. Shaughnessy** | Leading Western scholar of ancient China; co-editor of Cambridge History of Ancient China |
| **Lothar von Falkenhausen** | Key critic of the methodological nationalism in Chinese Bronze Age archaeology |

## Key Archaeological Sites

| Site | Location | Period | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zhoukoudian | Near Beijing, Hebei | 800,000–230,000 BP | Peking Man; earliest confirmed human habitation in China |
| Jiahu | Henan | c. 7000–5700 BCE | Early millet and rice cultivation; proto-writing symbols |
| Yangshao (Banpo) | Shaanxi | c. 5000–3000 BCE | Painted pottery culture; matrilineal society |
| Niuheliang | Liaoning | c. 3500–2700 BCE | Hongshan ritual complex; earliest Chinese jade |
| Liangzhu | Zhejiang | c. 3300–2300 BCE | UNESCO site; earliest Chinese state-level society; jade cong and bi |
| Longshan (Chengziya) | Shandong | c. 3000–1900 BCE | Black eggshell pottery; walled settlements; proto-urban society |
| Erlitou | Henan | c. 1900–1600 BCE | Possible Xia capital; earliest palace complexes; bronze casting |
| Yinxu / Anyang | Henan | c. 1300–1046 BCE | Last Shang capital; oracle bones; royal tombs; Fu Hao's tomb |
| Terracotta Army / Qin Tomb | Shaanxi (Xi'an) | c. 210 BCE | Burial complex of Qin Shi Huang; 8,000+ terracotta soldiers |
