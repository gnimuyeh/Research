# Ancient Chinese History: Learning Path

A step-by-step curriculum from complete beginner to advanced, with specific resources for each stage.

---

## Prerequisites (Before You Start)

No prior knowledge of Chinese history is required, but the following background will accelerate your learning:

- **Basic world history context:** Know roughly when other ancient civilizations existed (Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome) so you can situate China in comparative context.
- **Elementary geography:** Familiarize yourself with a map of China showing the Yellow River (Huang He), Yangtze River, the North China Plain, Shandong Peninsula, and the major modern provinces (Henan, Shaanxi, Shandong, Zhejiang, Sichuan).
- **No Chinese language required** for this curriculum, though learning to recognize key Chinese terms in pinyin will deepen your understanding.

---

## Stage 1: Orientation (2–4 weeks)

**Goal:** Get a vivid, continuous narrative of ancient Chinese history from beginning to end before diving into depth.

### Recommended Resources

1. **The Cambridge Illustrated History of China** by Patricia Buckley Ebrey (Cambridge UP, 2010)
   - Start with Chapters 1–5 (prehistory through Han)
   - Richly illustrated; combines narrative with archaeological evidence
   - Best single-volume orientation

2. **Khan Academy: Ancient China** (free, online)
   - Short videos and articles covering Shang, Zhou, Qin, Han
   - Good for visual learners; builds foundational vocabulary

3. **World History Encyclopedia articles** (free, online: worldhistory.org)
   - Read the articles on: Neolithic China, Shang Dynasty, Zhou Dynasty, Mandate of Heaven, Qin Dynasty, Han Dynasty, Confucianism, Oracle Bone Script
   - Peer-reviewed; well-sourced; suitable for serious beginners

4. **Smarthistory — Ancient China** (free, online: smarthistory.org)
   - Art-history focused but excellent for material culture and visual evidence
   - Particularly strong on Neolithic cultures, Shang bronzes, and Terracotta Army

### Key Concepts to Master at This Stage
- The basic dynastic sequence (see the timeline in `01-overview.md`)
- What the Yellow River and Yangtze River regions produced culturally
- The distinction between prehistoric, legendary, and historically attested periods
- What oracle bones are and why they matter

---

## Stage 2: Prehistoric and Legendary China (3–5 weeks)

**Goal:** Understand the Neolithic foundations and the legendary period with proper attention to what is myth vs. archaeology.

### Recommended Resources

1. **A Companion to Chinese Archaeology**, ed. Anne P. Underhill (Wiley-Blackwell, 2013)
   - Chapters on Yangshao, Longshan, Liangzhu, Hongshan cultures
   - Academic level; the best single resource for Neolithic China
   - Skip chapters on periods you haven't reached yet

2. **Neolithic in China — Wikipedia** (detailed summary, well-referenced)
   - Read thoroughly; follow footnotes to academic papers

3. **UNESCO World Heritage documentation:**
   - [Liangzhu City ruins](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1592/) — for the earliest state-level culture
   - [Yinxu / Anyang](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1114/) — for the Shang

4. **For the legendary period:**
   - **Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors — Wikipedia** (good summary of both the tradition and scholarly critique)
   - Gu Jiegang's "Doubting Antiquity" school framework: understand that later Chinese texts projected mythological figures backward onto early history

5. **For the Xia debate:**
   - Read the original (and accessible) research: Wu Qinglong et al., "Outburst Flood at 1920 BCE Supports Historicity of China's Great Flood and the Xia Dynasty," *Science* 353 (2016)
   - Read the counterpoint: Lothar von Falkenhausen, "Rethinking Erlitou: Legend, History and Chinese Archaeology," *Cambridge Archaeological Journal* (2003)

### Key Questions to Answer
- What archaeological evidence distinguishes the Yangshao, Longshan, and Liangzhu cultures?
- What does Erlitou show us archaeologically, regardless of whether it was the Xia capital?
- Why do Chinese and Western scholars disagree about the Xia?

---

## Stage 3: Shang Dynasty — China's Bronze Age (3–5 weeks)

**Goal:** Understand the Shang as a historically confirmed civilization and grasp the significance of oracle bone writing.

### Recommended Resources

1. **The Cambridge History of Ancient China**, ed. Loewe & Shaughnessy (Cambridge UP, 1999)
   - Chapters: "Shang Archaeology" and "The Shang: China's First Historical Dynasty"
   - This is the authoritative academic treatment; challenging but rewarding

2. **Smarthistory — Shang Dynasty**
   - Excellent on bronze vessels and material culture

3. **Primary source: Oracle Bone Inscriptions**
   - Browse the [CHANT Database](https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/ics/rccat/chant.html) (Chinese University of Hong Kong) for images of oracle bones
   - Read about the inscriptions in: [Sapiens — "The Chinese History That Is Written in Bone"](https://www.sapiens.org/archaeology/chinese-oracle-bones-history/)

4. **Britannica — Shang Dynasty** for factual overview
5. **Wikipedia — Yinxu** for the archaeology of the Shang capital

### Key Questions to Answer
- How did oracle bone divination work, and what can it tell us about Shang society?
- What role did the king play as both political and religious leader?
- What is the significance of the Tomb of Fu Hao, and what does it tell us about gender in Shang society?
- How did Shang bronze-casting technology compare to the ancient Near East?

---

## Stage 4: Zhou Dynasty — Feudalism, Philosophy, and Warfare (5–8 weeks)

**Goal:** This is the longest and richest period to study. Master both the political history and the philosophical legacy.

### Part A: Western Zhou (1046–771 BCE)

**Recommended Resources:**
1. **Cambridge History of Ancient China** — Western Zhou chapters
2. **"Society Under the Zhou Dynasty"** at Lumen Learning (free, online)
3. **Britannica — Zhou Feudal System**

**Key topics:** The Mandate of Heaven; Duke of Zhou; fengjian system; kinship-based governance.

### Part B: Spring and Autumn Period (771–476 BCE)

**Recommended Resources:**
1. **Primary source: *Chunqiu* (Spring and Autumn Annals)** — Read in translation (selections available in de Bary's *Sources of Chinese Tradition*)
2. **Confucius biography and the *Analects***:
   - Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — [Confucius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/) (best academic overview)
   - Read selections from the *Analects* in translation (D.C. Lau or Edward Slingerland translations recommended)
3. **Sun Tzu, *The Art of War*** — Read the full text (13 chapters; takes about 1 hour); any major translation. Samuel Griffith's (Oxford UP) includes excellent historical notes.

### Part C: Warring States Period (476–221 BCE) and Hundred Schools of Thought

**Recommended Resources:**
1. **Primary sources (all in translation):**
   - *Dao De Jing* — D.C. Lau or Stephen Mitchell translation
   - *Mozi* — selections in *Sources of Chinese Tradition*
   - *Han Feizi* — selections in *Sources of Chinese Tradition*
   - *Zhuangzi* — Burton Watson translation
2. **Secondary: "Hundred Schools of Thought — Wikipedia"** (excellent overview)
3. **Fiveable — Warring States Period** (good summary with exam focus)
4. **Wikipedia — Military of the Warring States** (for the military-technological revolution)
5. **Primary source: *I Ching* (Book of Changes)** — Read Richard Wilhelm's translation (Princeton/Bollingen) with introduction; understand it as a cosmological system, not just divination

### Key Questions to Answer
- How did the breakdown of the Zhou feudal system produce political chaos AND philosophical creativity simultaneously?
- What are the key differences between Confucian, Daoist, Mohist, and Legalist answers to the question: "How should society be governed?"
- How did iron weapons, the crossbow, and mass infantry change the nature of warfare in China?
- Why did none of the philosophical schools fully prevail? What was the compromise that eventually emerged?

---

## Stage 5: Qin Dynasty — Unification and Collapse (2–3 weeks)

**Goal:** Understand both the revolutionary achievements of the First Emperor and the structural reasons for Qin's rapid collapse.

### Recommended Resources

1. **Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand Historian: Qin Dynasty*** (Burton Watson translation, Columbia UP)
   - The primary ancient source; riveting narrative history
   - Includes the "Basic Annals of the First Emperor" — essential reading

2. **World History Encyclopedia — Qin Dynasty** (free online)

3. **Smithsonian Magazine — "What You Need to Know About China's Terra-Cotta Warriors"**
   - [Smithsonian — Terracotta Warriors](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/what-you-need-know-about-chinas-terra-cotta-warriors-first-qin-emperor-30942673/)

4. **Academia.edu — "Qin Shi Huang's Legacy"**
   - [Assessing the First Emperor's Impact](https://www.academia.edu/124381548/Qin_Shi_Huangs_Legacy_Assessing_the_First_Emperors_Impact_on_Chinese_History)

5. **Humanities LibreTexts — Qin Dynasty** (free, structured summary)

### Key Questions to Answer
- Why is Qin Shi Huang simultaneously celebrated as a great unifier and condemned as a tyrant?
- What specific Legalist policies created both Qin's strength and its brittleness?
- What is the significance of the standardization of writing beyond mere administrative convenience?
- Why did the world's first large-scale unified empire collapse within 15 years of its founder's death?

---

## Stage 6: Han Dynasty — Empire Consolidated (5–8 weeks)

**Goal:** Understand the Han as the period that created the enduring template of Chinese civilization.

### Part A: Western Han (206 BCE–9 CE)

**Recommended Resources:**
1. **Sima Qian, *Records of the Grand Historian: Han Dynasty*** (Burton Watson translation, 2 vols.)
   - The essential primary source; covers the founding through Emperor Wu
2. **Wikipedia — Emperor Wu of Han** (comprehensive summary of the most important Han emperor)
3. **History.com — Han Dynasty** (good narrative overview)
4. **Fiveable — Han Dynasty: Confucianism and Imperial Expansion**

### Part B: Wang Mang and Eastern Han (9–220 CE)

**Recommended Resources:**
1. **Ban Gu and Ban Zhao, *Hanshu* (Book of Han)** — key sections in English translation
   - The official Han history; crucial for Eastern Han events
2. **World History Encyclopedia — Han Dynasty** (covers full dynasty including fall)
3. **Khan Academy — Fall of the Han Dynasty**

### Part C: Han's Intellectual and Technological Legacy

**Key topics to research:**
- Paper (Cai Lun, 105 CE): [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/han-dynasty)
- Seismograph (Zhang Heng, 132 CE): search "Zhang Heng seismograph Han Dynasty"
- Sima Qian and Chinese historiography: see `02-foundational-papers.md`
- The Silk Road: read on Zhang Qian's missions (138 BCE)
- Dong Zhongshu's synthesis: "Disregard all other schools, exclusively honor Confucianism"

### Key Questions to Answer
- How did Emperor Wu transform both China's territory and its intellectual foundations?
- What is the Silk Road, and what traveled along it besides silk?
- Why did Wang Mang fail despite genuine reform intentions?
- What structural forces (court factionalism, eunuchs, regional warlords, peasant rebellion) ended the Han?
- Why does China's majority ethnic group still call itself "Han"?

---

## Stage 7: Advanced Study and Synthesis

### For Deeper Academic Engagement

1. **The Cambridge History of Ancient China** (full volume — now read entirely)
2. **Endymion Wilkinson, *Chinese History: A New Manual*** — use as reference guide
3. **Michael Loewe, *Early Chinese Texts: A Bibliographical Guide***
4. **Asian Studies academic journals:** *Journal of Asian Studies*, *T'oung Pao*, *Early China*, *Bulletin of the Museum of Far Eastern Antiquities*

### Comparative and Thematic Perspectives

5. **For comparative civilization:** Karl Jaspers' "Axial Age" concept — why did Confucius, Laozi, Buddha, Socrates, and the Hebrew prophets all live in the same era (800–200 BCE)?
6. **For political philosophy:** Compare Confucianism, Legalism, and Daoism as answers to the same governance problems; compare with Greek political philosophy of the same era
7. **For archaeology:** Anne Underhill's *Companion to Chinese Archaeology* (full volume)

### Suggested Sequence for Reading Primary Sources

| Order | Text | Translation Recommended |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Analects* | D.C. Lau (Penguin) or Edward Slingerland (Hackett) |
| 2 | *Art of War* | Samuel Griffith (Oxford UP) |
| 3 | *Dao De Jing* | D.C. Lau (Penguin) or Ursula Le Guin (Shambhala) |
| 4 | *Shiji* (selections) | Burton Watson (Columbia UP) — Qin Dynasty and Han Dynasty volumes |
| 5 | *Mozi* (selections) | Ian Johnston (Columbia UP) |
| 6 | *Han Feizi* (selections) | Burton Watson or W.K. Liao |
| 7 | *Zhuangzi* | Burton Watson (Columbia UP) |
| 8 | *I Ching* | Richard Wilhelm / Cary Baynes (Princeton UP) |
| 9 | *Hanshu* (selections) | Homer H. Dubs |

## Key Databases and Digital Resources

- **CHANT — Chinese Ancient Texts** (cuhk.edu.hk): Full-text database of pre-Han and Han texts including oracle bone inscriptions and bronze inscriptions
- **Scripta Sinica** (sinica.edu.tw): 688+ classical Chinese texts; freely accessible
- **Bibliography of Asian Studies**: Comprehensive academic citation database
- **Asia for Educators** (Columbia University, afe.easia.columbia.edu): Freely accessible lesson plans and primary source translations
- **Smarthistory** (smarthistory.org): Art history perspective; excellent images
- **World History Encyclopedia** (worldhistory.org): Peer-reviewed general reference
